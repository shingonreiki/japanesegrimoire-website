In anime they hang from school bags, swing from the rear-view mirror of a taxi, or get pressed into someone's hand before an exam. In Japan you can hardly walk a hundred meters without seeing one. An omamori (お守り) looks like a small decorative pouch, and that's exactly why most people outside Japan treat it like a souvenir. In Japan it's treated a bit more carefully.

What the word means

Mamoru means to protect, and omamori is, quite literally, "a protection," with the polite prefix o. Shrines and temples both give them out. At a Shintō shrine the omamori carries the power of the kami of that shrine. At a Buddhist temple it carries the power of a buddha, bodhisattva or protective being, often written as a Sanskrit seed syllable in the old Siddham script.

A small detail of language tells you a lot. In Japanese you don't really "buy" an omamori. You "receive" it, sazukaru, and the money you hand over is called an offering rather than a price. That's not just politeness. It reflects the idea that what you take home has been blessed and isn't a product.

What's inside

The brocade pouch is only the wrapping. Inside is usually a small slip of paper or a thin piece of wood with the name of the shrine or temple, the name of the kami or buddha, or a short prayer. It has been consecrated in a rite before it's given out. In other words, the omamori is a small, portable version of the larger paper talismans, the ofuda, that people keep at home. I write more about those in the article on ofuda.

And that's why you shouldn't open it. Opening an omamori is considered disrespectful, and many people believe that the protection leaves once the inner slip is exposed. Put simply, you'd be opening a sealed letter that wasn't addressed to you. If you're curious anyway, there are plenty of photos online. Your own one is better left closed.

The different kinds

Most shrines and temples offer a whole range, each one for a particular area of life. The name is usually embroidered on the front:

  • Kōtsū anzen (交通安全), safety on the road. The one you see hanging in cars.
  • Gakugyō jōju (学業成就), success in studies and exams. Very popular at shrines of Tenjin, the deified scholar Sugawara no Michizane.
  • En-musubi (縁結び), tying good bonds, mostly understood as love and finding a partner.
  • Anzan (安産), a safe birth.
  • Yakuyoke (厄除け), warding off misfortune, especially in the "unlucky years" of life, the yakudoshi.
  • Shōbai hanjō (商売繁盛), a thriving business. Inari shrines are the classic place for this one.
  • Kenkō (健康), good health, often linked at temples with the Medicine Buddha, Yakushi Nyorai.

It isn't magic in the sense of a guarantee. Japanese people would describe it more as a reminder and a support. You carry the blessing with you, and it keeps the wish present in your mind.

Your birth-year buddha

Here's a detail from esoteric Buddhism that most visitors never notice. Many Shingon temples offer omamori for your personal protective buddha, the mamori honzon (守り本尊), which depends on the zodiac animal of your birth year. The traditional assignment goes like this:

Yes, you read that right: the same twelve animals that Naruto turned into hand signs also decide which buddha watches over you. I explain the zodiac side in the article on Naruto's hand signs.

How to treat an omamori

There are no strict rules, but there are good customs:

  • Keep it with you or near the thing it protects, in your bag, your wallet, your car or at your desk.
  • Keep it clean and off the floor. It's a blessed object.
  • Don't open it. See above.
  • Several are fine. You can carry omamori from different shrines and temples at the same time.
  • Giving one as a gift is fine, and very common for exams, births and travel.

What to do after a year

The usual custom is to keep an omamori for about a year, or until the wish has come true. Then you bring it back, ideally to the place where you received it, and say thank you. Most shrines and temples have a box for returned omamori and ofuda. Around New Year and in mid-January, many of them burn the old ones in a large purification fire, in many regions called dondo-yaki, and the blessing goes back to where it came from. Throwing an omamori in the trash is the one thing you really shouldn't do.

If you can't get back to Japan, some shrines and temples accept returns by post, so it's worth checking their website. A shrine omamori traditionally goes back to a shrine and a temple omamori to a temple.
