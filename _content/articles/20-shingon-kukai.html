A lot of what anime presents as "Japanese magic," the hand seals, the chanted syllables, the circles of buddhas and the flames of fierce guardian kings, comes from one tradition. Most viewers have never heard its name. It's called Shingon, and it's the tradition at the center of my own research and practice.

A young man who walked out of university

Kūkai (空海) was born in 774 in Sanuki, on the island of Shikoku, today's Kagawa Prefecture. The temple Zentsū-ji stands where his family home is said to have been. As a gifted young man he was sent to the government university in the capital to become an official. He left. In a short work written in his twenties he explained why: Confucian learning and Daoism had their merits, but he had found something deeper in Buddhism.

He spent the following years as a wandering ascetic in the mountains of Shikoku and elsewhere. In a cave at Cape Muroto, at the southern tip of Shikoku, he practiced a demanding mantra recitation devoted to Kokūzō, the bodhisattva of boundless wisdom, and according to his own account the morning star entered his mouth. The name he took, Kūkai, means "sky and sea," and tradition says that's what he saw from the mouth of that cave: nothing but sky and sea.

Two years in China

In 804 Kūkai sailed to China with an official embassy. In the capital, Chang'an, he met the master Huiguo, the leading teacher of esoteric Buddhism of his time, who, according to Kūkai's report, greeted him with the words that he had been waiting for him. Within a few months Huiguo initiated him into both of the great mandalas and passed the full transmission to him. Huiguo died that same winter.

Kūkai returned to Japan in 806 with sutras, mandalas, ritual implements and a knowledge of Sanskrit and of the Siddham script (Bonji in Japanese) that almost nobody else in Japan had. While in China he would also have met Daoist thought and practice, and I think it's very likely that he knew exactly how closely the two traditions touched each other there.

What "Shingon" means

Shingon (真言) means "true word." It's the Japanese translation of the Sanskrit mantra. The name tells you where the emphasis lies. Shingon teaches that the cosmic buddha, Dainichi Nyorai (大日如来), the great sun buddha, is preaching all the time, through every sound, form and movement in the universe. Esoteric practice is a way of learning to hear and answer that.

The answer happens through the three mysteries, sanmitsu: the body forms a mudra, the voice speaks a mantra, the mind visualizes a buddha or its seed syllable. When all three are aligned with those of a buddha, Kūkai taught, a person can become a buddha in this very body, sokushin jōbutsu (即身成仏). Put simply, you don't have to wait for countless future lives. I write more about the hand seals in the article on mudras.

Two great mandalas map this universe. The Womb Realm mandala shows the compassion of the buddhas unfolding like a lotus. The Diamond Realm mandala shows their indestructible wisdom. They hang facing each other in Shingon halls, and practitioners work with both.

Kōyasan and Tō-ji

In 816 the emperor granted Kūkai a high plateau in the mountains south of Nara, Mount Kōya, for a monastery. In 823 he received the temple Tō-ji in Kyoto as a center in the capital. Kūkai was also an engineer who repaired a huge irrigation reservoir in his home province, a poet, and one of the three great calligraphers of his age. In Japan there's a saying that even Kōbō makes mistakes with the brush. If he can, so can you.

He died on Kōyasan in 835. In 921 the court gave him the title Kōbō Daishi, "Great Master who spread the Dharma." According to Shingon belief he didn't die but entered eternal meditation, and to this day monks bring him meals twice a day in the inner sanctuary, Okunoin, at the end of a path through a cedar forest lined with more than two hundred thousand graves and memorials.

My own path with Shingon

I studied East Asian art history and Japanese studies in Heidelberg. In Kyoto, where I did research for about three years with two scholarships, I spent much of my time in temples. There I came across the original form of a Reiki symbol written in Siddham, which became the subject of my master's thesis and later of my dissertation.

My professor in Kyoto recommended that I walk the Shikoku pilgrimage, the 88 temples connected with Kūkai. On my first attempt I gave up after about ten days, at Temple 24. A year later I walked all 88. He also gave me a statue of the Medicine Buddha. I'm a Shingon practitioner and researcher, and that's the root from which my work with Shingon Reiki and Kuji Kiri grows.

Where you can meet Shingon today

  • Kōyasan, Wakayama. Over a hundred temples on the mountain plateau, many offering overnight stays with vegetarian temple food and the morning service.
  • Tō-ji, Kyoto. The five-storied pagoda and the lecture hall with its statue mandala.
  • Zentsū-ji, Kagawa. Kūkai's birthplace, Temple 75 of the pilgrimage.
  • The 88 temples of Shikoku. All around the island, walked by pilgrims in white with a straw hat and a staff that stands for Kūkai himself.
