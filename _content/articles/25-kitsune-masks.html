A white face, narrow eyes, red lines like flames around them, sometimes a bit of gold. The fox mask is one of the most recognizable images of Japan, and in anime it's practically a shortcut for "something mysterious is about to happen." In Japan it's simply part of festival life. But the reason people put it on is older and more interesting than decoration.

The fox between worlds

To understand the mask, you have to understand the fox. In Japanese belief the fox has two faces. It's the messenger of the kami Inari, guarding shrines in stone with a key or a jewel in its mouth. And it's the shape-shifter of folklore, the clever trickster who takes human form. Inari itself isn't a fox, by the way, a common mistake I explain in the article on the nine-tailed fox.

What both kinds of fox share is that they move between worlds, between the fields and the village, between humans and kami, between night and day. Put simply, the fox is a being of the threshold. When you put on a fox mask, you borrow that quality for a while.

What the mask does

Masks in Japan have always done more than hide a face. In ritual dances and in theater, a performer who puts on a mask is understood to take on the character or the being it shows. In festivals, wearing a mask lets ordinary people step out of their everyday role. The shopkeeper and the schoolgirl become foxes for an evening, and the usual rules relax a little. That's exactly why masks belong to festivals, the times of the year when the boundary between the human world and the world of the kami is thought to be open.

Fox weddings and foxfires

Two old folk images explain a lot of today's fox-mask customs.

The first is kitsune no yomeiri (狐の嫁入り), the fox's wedding. People told of mysterious rows of lights seen in the hills at night and explained them as a procession of foxes escorting a bride. In Japanese the same phrase also means a sunshower, rain falling from a clear sky, because the foxes were said to hold their weddings when the weather played tricks. In Tsugawa, in Niigata Prefecture, the town has turned the legend into a spring festival, with a fox wedding procession of people in fox makeup walking through the town and across the river at dusk.

The second is the foxfire of Ōji in Tokyo. According to Edo-period legend, on New Year's Eve the foxes of the whole Kantō region gathered under a great tree near Ōji Inari Shrine, dressed in their finest, before paying their respects to the shrine. Farmers counted their ghostly fires to predict the coming harvest. The woodblock artist Hiroshige made a famous print of the scene in 1857. Since the 1990s, the neighborhood holds a fox procession every New Year's Eve, with hundreds of people in fox masks and fox makeup walking to the shrine at midnight. It's one of the most memorable ways to spend the last night of the year in Tokyo.

Foxes on stage

The fox also has a long life in theater. In the kyōgen comedy Tsurigitsune, an old fox disguises himself as a priest to talk a hunter out of trapping foxes, and it's considered one of the most demanding roles of the repertoire. In kabuki, fox characters often jump, twist and move in a way no human would, which is half the fun. Some kagura dances at shrines also include fox masks, especially in Inari rites.

The festival stall

The fox mask most visitors take home comes from a festival stall. At summer festivals and shrine fairs you'll find a booth hung with masks: foxes, hyottoko and okame with their round comic faces, tengu with long red noses, and nowadays a fair number of anime characters. Traditional masks are made of papier-mâché or wood and painted by hand, and the shops around Fushimi Inari in Kyoto sell them all year round. There's no single correct design. The red lines, the gold and sometimes a small flame or jewel on the forehead vary from maker to maker.

Demon Slayer's fox masks

In Demon Slayer, the old master Urokodaki carves a fox mask for each of his students as a charm against misfortune. Tanjiro receives one too. It's a lovely detail, because it combines two real Japanese ideas: masks as protection, and the fox as a being who stands between worlds and watches over those who cross. Where you can see fox masks today

  • Ōji Inari Shrine, Tokyo, on New Year's Eve. The fox procession at midnight.
  • Tsugawa, Niigata, in spring. The fox wedding procession by the river.
  • Fushimi Inari Taisha, Kyoto. Mask shops along the approach, and fox-face votive tablets to draw on.
  • Any summer festival. Look for the mask stall between the goldfish scooping and the shaved ice.
