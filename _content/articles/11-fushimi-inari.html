Most people know Fushimi Inari as a photo. A tunnel of orange gates, a little mist, ideally nobody else in the frame. That last part takes some planning, because this is one of the most visited places in Japan. But the gates aren't there for the photo, and they weren't put up by an architect. Every single one of them has a story, and most of those stories begin with a wish.

A rice cake that flew away

Fushimi Inari Taisha (伏見稲荷大社) lies in the south of Kyoto at the foot of Mount Inari. According to the shrine's own tradition it was founded in 711. An old local record tells the founding legend. A wealthy man of the Hata clan, a powerful immigrant family in the Kyoto basin, used a rice cake as an archery target. The rice cake turned into a white bird, flew off, and came down on the mountain, where rice began to grow. The place was called ina-nari, "where the rice grows," and that's one of the explanations given for the name Inari.

It's a story about respect for food, and about the idea that the power of growth can't be owned. You can treat it as a fable. In Japan, people have treated it as the foundation of one of the biggest networks of shrines in the country. Fushimi Inari is the head shrine of around thirty thousand Inari shrines throughout Japan.

Inari and the foxes

Inari Ōkami is the kami of rice, harvest and prosperity, and by extension of business success. The foxes you'll see everywhere on the mountain are the messengers. They carry keys, jewels, scrolls and ears of rice in their mouths. I've explained the difference, and the whole fox lore that comes with it, in the article on the nine-tailed fox. One small detail is worth looking for here: many of the votive tablets at Fushimi Inari are shaped like fox heads, and visitors draw a face on them. Some are serious, many are funny, and quite a few look like anime characters.

There's also a Buddhist side to the story. For centuries Fushimi Inari served as the protective shrine of Tō-ji, the great temple that the emperor gave to Kūkai in 823. According to tradition, Kūkai himself honored Inari as a protector of his temple, and during Tō-ji's spring festival the portable shrines of Inari still pass by the temple. In Japan kami and buddhas lived side by side for over a thousand years, and here you can still see it.

Why there are ten thousand gates

A torii (鳥居) marks the entrance to sacred ground. Most shrines have one or two. Fushimi Inari has roughly ten thousand, and the reason is quite practical. Each one was donated. People and companies give a torii to the shrine in gratitude for a wish that has come true, or in the hope that one will. Since Inari is the kami of prosperity, a lot of businesses do this, from small family shops to large corporations.

Walk up through the famous double row called Senbon Torii, the "thousand torii," and then turn around. On the back of each pillar, the side you see on the way down, you'll find the name of the donor and the date in black characters. In other words, the tunnel is a huge, living list of thank-you notes, and it's renewed all the time, because the wood weathers and old gates are replaced by new ones.

The color has its own meaning. The vermilion red, shu, is considered protective, and it's associated with the power of life and of the harvest. The paint traditionally also preserved the wood, which is a very Japanese way of combining the sacred and the practical.

What most visitors miss

Most people walk the first few hundred meters of gates, take their pictures and turn back. That's a pity, because the mountain itself is the real shrine. The full loop to the summit, at about 233 meters, and back down takes two to three hours on foot. The crowds thin out quickly once you climb.

On the way you'll find:

  • The heavy-light stones. At the inner shrine, Okusha Hōhaisho, there are two stone lanterns with round stones on top. You make a wish and lift the stone. If it feels lighter than you expected, the wish is said to come true. If it's heavier, you'll need more patience.
  • The otsuka. Higher up, the paths are lined with thousands of small stone mounds and altars, each with its own name for Inari, set up by families, groups and individuals. That's where you see how personal this faith is. Many people have their very own form of Inari.
  • The view. At the Yotsutsuji crossing, about halfway, you look out over the whole of Kyoto. At sunset it's one of the most beautiful views of the city.
  • Small tea houses. Old family stands along the path sell kitsune udon with fried tofu, said to be the foxes' favorite food, and inari-zushi, rice in fried tofu pockets.

How to visit with respect

You don't need to be a believer to visit. A few small things go a long way, though. Bow briefly before you pass through the first large torii. Walk a little to the side of the path, because the middle is said to belong to the kami. At the main hall you can make a short prayer: put a coin in the box, bow twice, clap twice, and bow once more. Please don't climb on the fox statues, and don't carve anything into the torii. They are thank-you notes, after all.

If you go early in the morning or after dark, you'll have the gates mostly to yourself. Fushimi Inari is open around the clock, and at night, with the lanterns lit, the mountain feels very different.
