Esoteric Japan

Mountain ascetics, court diviners, paper talismans and the secret teachings of Shingon. The spiritual traditions that Japan's stories keep drawing from.

The character 密 (mitsu) means secret, or dense, or intimate. It's the first half of mikkyō, the esoteric Buddhism that Kūkai brought from China to Japan in the early ninth century and that lives on in the Shingon school. I spent about three years researching its art, architecture and rituals at Kyoto University, and I still practice it.

Esoteric Japan is bigger than one school, though. In Japan the old is rarely thrown away when something new arrives. It lives on next to it and slowly blends in. So you find Shingon next to Shintō, the mountain practice of Shugendō, the court diviners of onmyōdō with their roots in Chinese Daoism, and a whole world of amulets, talismans and purification rites that ordinary people use every day.

This section explains where these traditions come from and what they mean. How to practice them is a different matter, and that part is passed on in person.
