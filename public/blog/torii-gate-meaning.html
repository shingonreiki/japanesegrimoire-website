Torii Gates: Meaning, Colors and How to Pass Through One

You've seen a torii at the start of Spirited Away, telling you the world is about to change. In Japan there are tens of thousands of them, and each one says the same thing: from here on, you're on sacred ground.

By Dr. Mark Hosak · October 30, 2026 · 4 min read

At the very beginning of Spirited Away, before Chihiro's family finds the tunnel, the car passes an old, moss-covered torii in the forest, with small stone shrines gathered beneath a tree. Most viewers barely notice it. Japanese viewers know immediately what it means. We're crossing a line, and on the other side the rules are different.

A gate without a door

A torii (鳥居) marks the entrance to the sacred ground of a shrine, the space where a kami is present. It's a simple structure: two upright pillars, a crossbeam on top, usually a second crossbeam below it, and often a small plaque in the middle with the name of the shrine.

What makes it special is what it doesn't do. A torii has no door. It doesn't keep anyone out, and you can usually walk around it. Put simply, it's a sign that tells you where you are. Its relative is the sacred straw rope, the shimenawa, which marks a tree, a rock or a waterfall as the dwelling of a kami. Both say the same thing: pay attention here.

Where the name comes from

Nobody knows for certain, and I'd rather lay out the theories than pretend otherwise. The characters mean something like "bird" and "to be," which led to the popular explanation that the torii was a perch for birds. That's often linked to the myth of the sun hiding in the rock cave, where the kami set roosters crowing to lure Amaterasu out. Another theory derives the word from tōri-iru, "to pass through and enter." Others point to similar gateways in other parts of Asia. None of these is proven, and the question will probably stay open.

Two main styles

There are dozens of types, but almost all belong to one of two families:

  • Shinmei torii (神明鳥居). Straight, simple and severe. The top beam is straight, and the lower beam doesn't stick out past the pillars. The torii of Ise Jingū, made of plain unpainted cypress, are the classic example.
  • Myōjin torii (明神鳥居). The top beam curves gently upward at the ends, and the lower beam sticks out on both sides. This is the type most people picture, usually painted vermilion.

Some variants are worth knowing. The ryōbu torii, with small extra legs supporting each pillar, is the type of the great floating torii of Miyajima. Its name hints at the old combination of Shintō and esoteric Buddhism. The sannō torii has a triangular gable on top, typical of the Hiyoshi shrines near Mount Hiei. And at Konoshima Shrine in Kyoto stands a rare three-legged torii, three gates joined in a triangle around a sacred spring.

Why so many are vermilion

The vermilion red, shu (朱), is associated with warding off harmful influences, with life force and with the sun. The traditional pigment, made from cinnabar, also protected the wood from rot and insects. That's a very Japanese combination of the sacred and the practical.

But red isn't required. The torii of Ise are plain wood, renewed with the shrine buildings every twenty years. Many torii are made of stone, bronze or, in modern times, concrete. At Nonomiya Shrine in the bamboo grove of Arashiyama stands a black torii made of oak logs with the bark still on, one of the oldest forms. Each material says something about the shrine behind it.

Torii at Buddhist temples

Here's a detail most guidebooks skip. You'll find torii at Buddhist temples, too. Shitennō-ji in Osaka, one of the oldest temples in Japan, has a stone torii from 1294 at its western entrance. For more than a thousand years, kami and buddhas were worshipped together, often in the same compounds. When the government separated them officially in 1868, many torii simply stayed where they were. They're a reminder that in Japan the sacred was never neatly divided.

The floating torii of Miyajima

The most famous torii in Japan stands in the sea in front of Itsukushima Shrine on the island of Miyajima, near Hiroshima. At high tide it seems to float. At low tide you can walk out to it on the sand. The present gate dates from 1875 and is about sixteen meters tall. Remarkably, it isn't sunk into the seabed. It stands by its own weight, with its top beam filled with stones to make it heavier. Whole generations of pilgrims approached the shrine by boat, passing through the torii from the sea.

How to pass through a torii

None of this is required for visitors, but it's appreciated:

  • Bow briefly before you step through the first torii of a shrine.
  • Walk a little to the side. The center of the path, seichū, is said to belong to the kami.
  • Take off your hat if you're wearing one.
  • When you leave, turn around after passing through and bow once more toward the shrine.

That's all. It takes three seconds, and it shows that you know where you are. If you want to see what happens when people donate thousands of torii to one mountain, have a look at the article on Fushimi Inari.

Where to see remarkable torii

  • Itsukushima Shrine, Miyajima. The floating torii, best at high tide around sunset.
  • Fushimi Inari Taisha, Kyoto. About ten thousand torii on one mountain.
  • Ise Jingū, Mie. The plain wooden torii of Japan's most important shrine.
  • Nonomiya Shrine, Kyoto. The black torii of unbarked logs.
  • Shitennō-ji, Osaka. The stone torii of a Buddhist temple.

Quick answers

What does a torii gate mean?

A torii marks the entrance to the sacred ground of a kami. Passing through it means stepping out of ordinary space into a space where a kami is present. It has no door and doesn't keep anyone out.

Why are torii red?

Many torii are painted vermilion (shu), a color associated with warding off evil, with life force and with the sun. The pigment also protected the wood. Not all torii are red: the torii of Ise are plain unpainted wood, and many are stone, bronze or concrete.

How do you walk through a torii?

It's customary to bow briefly before passing through, to walk a little to the side because the center of the path is said to belong to the kami, and to turn and bow again when you leave.

Are there torii at Buddhist temples?

Yes. Because kami and buddhas were worshipped together for more than a thousand years, many temples have torii. Shitennō-ji in Osaka has a stone torii from 1294.
