The Nine-Tailed Fox: Kitsune in Japanese Folklore and the Inari Faith

You've seen the Nine-Tails sealed inside Naruto. Japan's own nine-tailed fox is older, more elegant and, according to the stories, brought down three kingdoms.

By Dr. Mark Hosak · October 30, 2026 · 4 min read

In Naruto the Nine-Tails is a giant beast of chakra and rage. In Japanese legend the nine-tailed fox is something quite different. She is beautiful, extremely clever, very old, and she usually works from inside the palace rather than smashing it from outside. Before we get to her, though, one misunderstanding needs clearing up, because it's the most common mistake people make about Japanese foxes.

Inari is not a fox

Japan has more shrines for Inari than for almost any other kami, around thirty thousand by the shrines' own count. You recognize them by their vermilion torii and by the pair of stone foxes at the entrance. That's why many people call Inari "the fox god." A priest at Fushimi Inari Taisha in Kyoto would politely correct you.

Inari Ōkami is a kami of rice, harvest and prosperity, often identified with Uka-no-Mitama. The foxes are the messengers, shinshi (神使) or otsukai (お使い). Japan has several of these messenger animals: deer at Kasuga in Nara, crows at Kumano, doves at Hachiman shrines, foxes at Inari. Put simply, the fox carries prayers and blessings between people and the kami. It serves Inari and doesn't replace Inari.

Look closely at the stone foxes next time. They usually hold something in their mouths: a key, a jewel, a scroll or an ear of rice. The key is generally explained as the key to the rice storehouse, the jewel as the wish-fulfilling jewel of Buddhism. And here you can already see two traditions meeting. Inari was worshipped side by side with buddhas for most of Japanese history. According to tradition, Kūkai made Inari a protector of his temple Tō-ji in Kyoto, and at Toyokawa Inari in Aichi, which is a Zen temple, the main object of worship is Dakiniten, a protective being from esoteric Buddhism who rides a white fox.

Good foxes, wild foxes

Folklore adds a second kind of fox. Next to the messengers of Inari there are the wild foxes of the fields and forests, who can take human shape, usually as a beautiful woman, and play tricks on people. Some stories are funny, some are sad, and a few are love stories. The most famous of those is the tale of Kuzunoha, the fox wife who married a man, bore him a son and had to leave when her true nature was discovered. That son is said to have been Abe no Seimei, the most famous court diviner in Japanese history. I tell more of that in the article on onmyōdō and Abe no Seimei.

The basic rule in the stories is that a fox gains power with age. The older it gets, the more tails it grows, and when it reaches nine, often said to happen after a thousand years, it has reached the peak of its power. A nine-tailed fox, kyūbi no kitsune (九尾の狐), can be a heavenly being of great wisdom or, as in the most famous legend, something far more dangerous.

Where the nine-tailed fox comes from

The nine-tailed fox first appears in ancient China. In the oldest texts it is a strange creature of a distant mountain, and depending on the source it is a dangerous man-eater or a good omen that appears when a ruler governs well. Later Chinese literature connected it with Daji, the consort of the last king of the Shang dynasty, whose cruelty was blamed on a fox spirit that had possessed her.

Tamamo-no-Mae and the Killing Stone

Japan took this figure and gave it its own great legend. The versions differ in their details, and the earliest written forms date from the Muromachi period, but the outline goes like this.

In the twelfth century a young woman of astonishing beauty and learning appears at the imperial court. Her name is Tamamo-no-Mae. She can answer any question, the emperor falls in love with her, and soon afterwards he falls ill. No physician can find the cause. Finally the court diviner Abe no Yasuchika, a descendant of Abe no Seimei, sees through her. She is a nine-tailed fox who had already brought down rulers in China and India, and now she has come to Japan. Exposed, she flees to the plain of Nasu in the east, where warriors hunt her down and kill her.

That's not quite the end. Her spirit passes into a large rock, the Sesshō-seki (殺生石), the Killing Stone, which kills every bird and animal that comes too close. Centuries later the Buddhist monk Gennō pacifies the spirit with a memorial rite. The story became a Noh play, and Edo-period woodblock artists loved it, especially the version where the fox travels through three countries.

Here comes the part that sounds like fiction. The Sesshō-seki is real. It stands at Nasu-Yumoto in Tochigi Prefecture, in a volcanic area where toxic gases really do come out of the ground, which explains the dead birds very well. In March 2022 the stone split in two. The cracks had been there for years, so it was almost certainly natural, but more than a few people in Japan joked that the fox had just been set free. I'll leave it to you which explanation you prefer.

Where you can meet the foxes today

  • Fushimi Inari Taisha, Kyoto. The head shrine of all Inari shrines, with its tunnels of roughly ten thousand torii running up the mountain and fox statues at every turn.
  • Any small Inari shrine. On rooftops, in back alleys, between rice fields. Japan is full of them.
  • Toyokawa Inari, Aichi. A hillside full of stone foxes and Dakiniten riding a white fox, a good place to see Buddhism and Shintō side by side.
  • The Sesshō-seki at Nasu, Tochigi. The Killing Stone, now in two halves, surrounded by small stone figures and the smell of sulfur.

Quick answers

Is Inari a fox?

No. Inari is a kami of rice, harvest and prosperity. The foxes at Inari shrines are Inari's messengers, called shinshi or otsukai. Calling Inari 'the fox god' is a common mistake.

Where does the nine-tailed fox come from?

The nine-tailed fox first appears in ancient Chinese texts. In Japan it became famous through the legend of Tamamo-no-Mae, a fox spirit who appeared at the imperial court in the twelfth century and, in the later versions of the story, had already ruined rulers in China and India.

What is the Sesshō-seki?

The Sesshō-seki, or Killing Stone, is a volcanic rock at Nasu in Tochigi Prefecture. According to legend the spirit of the nine-tailed fox Tamamo-no-Mae lived on inside it. The stone split in two in March 2022.

How does a kitsune get nine tails?

In Japanese folklore a fox grows more powerful with age and gains more tails. A fox with nine tails is very old, often said to be a thousand years, and has reached the height of its power.
