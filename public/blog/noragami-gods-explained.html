Noragami's Gods Explained: Yato, Bishamonten and Tenjin in Real Japanese Belief

You've seen Yato hustling for five-yen coins and dreaming of his own shrine. Noragami's idea that a kami needs people who remember him comes straight from Japanese religion, and so do Bishamon, Tenjin, Ebisu and Daikoku.

By Dr. Mark Hosak · October 30, 2026 · 4 min read

Noragami begins with a kami who has nothing: no shrine, no worshippers, a phone number scrawled on walls and a fee of five yen per job. Yato wants a shrine of his own, and he's terrified of being forgotten, because in the world of the series a kami whom nobody remembers simply disappears. It's a funny premise and a sad one. It's also surprisingly close to how many Japanese people have thought about their kami.

A kami needs people

In Japanese religion the relationship between kami and people goes both ways. People bring offerings, keep the shrine clean, hold festivals. The kami, in return, protect the village, the harvest, the family. A shrine that nobody visits any more is a sad sight in Japan, and with rural depopulation there are more of them every year.

The folklorist Yanagita Kunio suggested that many yokai were once kami who had lost their worshippers. That fits Noragami's world remarkably well. A forgotten power doesn't just vanish. It can become unpredictable, even dangerous, which is exactly the shadow that hangs over Yato's past as a god of calamity.

Five yen

Yato asks for five yen per job. That's not just a joke about being cheap. In Japanese, five yen is go-en (五円), which sounds the same as go-en (御縁), a bond or a good connection. That's why five-yen coins are the most popular offering at shrines. When Hiyori gives Yato five yen, she's quite literally offering him a bond.

Bishamon, the armored guardian

In the series, Bishamon is a fierce female warrior with a whole household of spirit weapons. The real Bishamonten (毘沙門天) is a Buddhist protective being, not a kami, and in Japanese tradition he's always depicted as a man in armor. He's the guardian of the north among the Four Heavenly Kings, the shitennō, who stand at the four corners of the altar in many temples. Under the name Tamonten, "the one who hears much," he's the most prominent of the four.

You'll recognize him by what he holds: a small pagoda in one hand and a spear or halberd in the other. Warriors loved him. The sixteenth-century warlord Uesugi Kenshin carried the first character of Bishamonten's name, 毘, on his banners. At Kurama north of Kyoto, Bishamonten is one of the three spirits honored together at the temple, which I describe in the article on Kurama. And at Chōgo-sonshi-ji on Mount Shigi near Nara, he's linked with the tiger, so the temple grounds are full of tiger statues.

Making Bishamon the strongest fighter in the series isn't far off, then. The gender swap is the author's own idea.

Tenjin, the scholar who became a kami

Noragami's Tenjin is a dignified old man who loves plum blossoms. That's right on target. The real Tenjin is Sugawara no Michizane (845–903), one of the most brilliant scholars and statesmen of the Heian court. Falsely accused by rivals, he was exiled to Dazaifu in Kyushu, where he died. Soon afterward lightning struck the palace, his enemies died one after another, and the capital was terrified. The court restored his rank and enshrined him as a kami, and so a vengeful spirit became the kami of learning. I tell more about such spirits in the article on Japanese ghost stories.

The plum blossoms come from a famous legend. Michizane wrote a farewell poem to the plum tree in his garden in Kyoto, and the tree is said to have flown after him all the way to Dazaifu. That tree, the "flying plum," still stands at Dazaifu Tenmangū. Today students all over Japan pray to Tenjin before their entrance exams, and the shrines are covered in votive tablets with exam wishes every winter.

Ebisu and Daikoku

Two more of the series' figures belong to the Shichifukujin, the seven beings of good fortune, usually called the Seven Lucky Gods in English. They're a typically Japanese mix. Ebisu is a kami, the only one of the seven who is originally Japanese, a cheerful fisherman with a fishing rod and a big sea bream, the patron of fishermen and merchants. His main shrine is Nishinomiya in Hyōgo. Daikokuten came from India as a dark protective being and turned in Japan into a smiling figure standing on rice bales with a sack and a little mallet that shakes out treasure. He was also linked with the kami Ōkuninushi of Izumo, because both names can be read "Daikoku."

The other five are Bishamonten, Benzaiten, Fukurokuju, Jurōjin and Hotei. Together they ride the treasure ship that people put under their pillow at New Year to dream lucky dreams.

Kofuku and the god of poverty

Kofuku, cheerful and destructive, is a binbōgami (貧乏神), a god of poverty. That's real folk belief too. A binbōgami was said to move into a house and drag the family down, and Edo-period stories tell of people trying to get rid of theirs, sometimes by treating it so well that it left happy. Pairing such a figure with Daikoku, the bringer of wealth, is one of the series' best jokes.

Where you can meet them today

  • Kitano Tenmangū, Kyoto, and Dazaifu Tenmangū, Fukuoka. Tenjin's great shrines, with thousands of plum trees blooming in February.
  • Chōgo-sonshi-ji, Nara. Bishamonten and his tigers.
  • Nishinomiya Shrine, Hyōgo. Ebisu's head shrine, with a famous race of young men in January.
  • Seven Lucky Gods pilgrimages. Small circuits of seven shrines and temples in many cities, very popular in the first week of the year.

Quick answers

Is Yato a real Japanese god?

No. Yato is an original character of the series. But the idea behind him, that a kami without worshippers fades and a forgotten one can become dangerous, reflects real Japanese belief.

Who is Bishamonten?

Bishamonten (毘沙門天), also called Tamonten, is a Buddhist protective being, the armored guardian of the north and one of the Four Heavenly Kings. He usually holds a small pagoda and a spear. In Japan he is also one of the Seven Lucky Gods. In real tradition he is depicted as male.

Who is Tenjin?

Tenjin is the deified scholar and statesman Sugawara no Michizane (845–903). After his death in exile, disasters in the capital were blamed on his angry spirit, and he was enshrined as a kami. Today he is the kami of learning, honored at Kitano Tenmangū in Kyoto and Dazaifu Tenmangū in Fukuoka.

Why do people offer five-yen coins at shrines?

Five yen is go-en, which sounds like go-en, a good bond or connection. Offering it is a small wish for a good relationship with the kami.
