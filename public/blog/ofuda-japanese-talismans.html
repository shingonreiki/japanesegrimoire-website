Ofuda and Japanese Talismans: Paper Protection from Shrines and Temples

You've seen exorcists slap paper seals on demons in Inuyasha and Jujutsu Kaisen. In Japan those paper talismans hang above front doors and stand on household shelves, and some of them are drawn with crows.

By Dr. Mark Hosak · October 30, 2026 · 4 min read

In anime the paper talisman is a weapon. A priestess throws it, an exorcist slaps it onto a demon's forehead, and the monster freezes. In Japan the same kind of paper is far less dramatic and far more common. It sits on a little shelf in the living room, hangs above the kitchen door or is glued to the entrance of a shop. Many Japanese households have one.

What an ofuda is

An ofuda (お札) is a talisman issued by a shrine or a temple. It's usually a strip of thick paper, sometimes a thin board of wood, often wrapped in white paper. On it you find the name of the shrine or temple, the name of a kami or buddha, a seal stamped in red, and sometimes a picture or a Sanskrit seed syllable in Siddham script (Bonji in Japanese). Other words for it are gofu (護符), protective charm, and at shrines shinsatsu (神札), kami talisman.

The difference from an omamori is simple. An ofuda protects a place and stays there. An omamori, which I've written about in its own article, is the small portable version that you carry with you.

Where paper talismans come from

Written talismans have deep roots in Chinese Daoism, where specialists drew charms in a special script to summon protective powers and drive out harmful ones. That's also where the image of the paper seal on a hopping vampire's forehead in Hong Kong films comes from. This knowledge came to Japan together with yin-yang lore and esoteric Buddhism. In the Heian period the court diviners of onmyōdō made amulets and protective charms as part of their official duties, which I describe in the article on Abe no Seimei.

Kūkai, who brought Shingon from China in the early ninth century, had studied both esoteric Buddhism and Daoist thought there, and I think it's very likely that he knew exactly how the two were connected. In Japan the talisman then went its own way again. Shrines issued ofuda for their kami, temples for their buddhas, and the mountain ascetics of Shugendō carried protective charms into every village they passed through. As so often in Japan, nothing replaced anything else. It all simply piled up.

Four talismans worth knowing

Jingū Taima, the talisman of Ise

The most widespread ofuda in Japan comes from the Grand Shrine of Ise, the shrine of the sun kami Amaterasu. In the Edo period, shrine agents traveled the whole country distributing Ise talismans, and huge waves of pilgrims walked to Ise in return. In 1867 rumors spread that Ise talismans were falling from the sky, and crowds danced through the streets shouting "Ee ja nai ka," roughly "Isn't it great?" Today the Jingū Taima is distributed through local shrines and is the classic centerpiece of a household shrine.

Goō hōin, the crow talisman of Kumano

This one is remarkable. The three great shrines of Kumano on the Kii Peninsula issue a talisman whose characters are drawn entirely out of little crows, dozens of them. Crows are the messengers of Kumano, and the three-legged crow Yatagarasu is said to have guided the legendary first emperor through the mountains. In the Middle Ages, samurai wrote their sworn oaths on the back of these crow talismans. According to a popular belief, each time someone broke such an oath, a crow at Kumano died. That's a strong argument for keeping your word.

Sominsōrai, the gift for a kind host

A legend tells of a traveling deity who asked two brothers for shelter. The rich brother refused. The poor one, Sominsōrai, welcomed him with what little he had. In return the deity promised that anyone who showed they were Sominsōrai's descendants would be spared from epidemics. That's why talismans and wooden charms reading "descendants of Sominsōrai" are still hung at entrances in many regions, and in Kyoto the charms of the Gion festival carry the same promise. The deity in the story is linked to Gozu Tennō and Susanoo, the kami of Yasaka Shrine.

Tsuno Daishi, the horned great master

In Kyoto you'll sometimes see a strange picture pasted above a door: a skinny, horned demon-like figure. It's the Tendai monk Ryōgen of the tenth century, who, according to legend, took on the form of a demon in meditation to drive out a plague. His image is issued by temples of the Tendai school and protects the house against illness and misfortune.

How ofuda are kept

At home, an ofuda usually stands on a kamidana (神棚), a small shelf shaped like a miniature shrine, mounted high on the wall in a clean, quiet room, traditionally facing south or east. Buddhist talismans can go on the household altar or on the wall. Some ofuda belong above the front door, the kitchen or the entrance of a business.

Like omamori, ofuda are traditionally renewed once a year. Around New Year you take the old one back to the shrine or temple, give thanks, and receive a new one. The returned talismans are burned in a purification fire in January. Nobody expects a visitor from abroad to set up a kamidana, of course. If you bring an ofuda home, a clean, high place where it isn't forgotten behind the TV is a respectful choice.

On the Shikoku pilgrimage you'll see the custom from the other side, by the way. Pilgrims leave a small name slip, an osamefuda, at every one of the 88 temples. The talisman goes in both directions there.

Quick answers

What is an ofuda?

An ofuda (お札) is a talisman of paper or wood issued by a Shintō shrine or a Buddhist temple. It carries the name or power of a kami or buddha and is kept at home, usually on a household shrine shelf, or attached above a door.

What is the difference between ofuda and omamori?

An ofuda protects a place, such as a house, a room or a business, and stays there. An omamori is a small portable version, wrapped in a pouch, that protects the person who carries it.

Where do Japanese paper talismans come from?

Written talismans have Chinese Daoist roots and came to Japan together with yin-yang lore and esoteric Buddhism. In Japan they developed further at shrines, temples and in the mountain practice of Shugendō.

How long do you keep an ofuda?

Usually about a year. At New Year the old ofuda is returned to the shrine or temple with thanks and a new one is received. Returned ofuda are burned in a purification fire.
